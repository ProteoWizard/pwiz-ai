Osprey demultiplexing through ProteoWizard

The alternative to pwiz PR #4710: one demultiplexer, in ProteoWizard, shared by msconvert, Skyline and Osprey. Updated 2026-10-12: #4710's gain attributed to its Poisson-weighted refit and ported into ProteoWizard (#4827) with makima, replicated on Mike's data and library and on a second set, and timed on an idle machine. Sources: TODO-20261008_osprey_pwiz_demux.md, pr4710-comparison.md, pr1b-root-cause.md in pwiz-ai, and D:\test\osprey-runs\pwiz-demux\attrib\FINDINGS.md on BRENDANX-i9.

The proposal. #4710 adds staggered-window and MSX demultiplexing to Osprey with its own engine (Osprey.Demux, Osprey.DemuxTool; about 9,600 added lines of product code). ProteoWizard already has a demultiplexer, the one msconvert users rely on. Making it correct, fast and reachable through MsDataFileImpl lets Osprey's --demux auto be 559 added lines of Osprey product code (plus the pwiz PRs, shared by every tool). Mike's improvements come with it: the Poisson-weighted refit that produces #4710's gain, and makima interpolation, are now ProteoWizard options (#4827). With both, the integrated path identifies as many precursors as #4710 on Mike's data and library and 5% more on a second set, in less than half the time and memory.

What produces #4710's gain: its Poisson-weighted refit

Each of the weighted engine's options was switched off in turn (a measurement build of #4710 overriding its descriptor settings), checked first on the EV13 spectra and then by search where the spectra moved:

  • Poisson-weighted refit (after the NNLS, a refit with row weights 1 / max(A x, floor)): switched off, the weighted engine finds 42,260, ProteoWizard's 42,188 to within 0.2%. It is the whole gain. Ported into ProteoWizard's NNLS it gives +2.3% and +2.4% on the two sets. The floor does not matter between 50 and 5,000 intensity units (Orbitrap centroids start at about 1,350).
  • Fragment channels found across a 20-cycle block, pass-through of weak channels, block and group sizes, and the output threshold: no measurable change in the spectra.
  • #4710's msconvert-style engine in msconvert's settings matches ProteoWizard (42,130 vs 42,188, three seeds), so the comparison itself is sound. No single one of its knobs (covered bins, makima, solution output) moves the count, and its default's +1.4% fell to +0.3% with three fresh seeds: seed luck.
  • Makima alone was +0.5%, inside the seed spread; on top of the Poisson refit it adds +0.8% and +1.3%.

Makima on real data

The case for makima rested on modeled apex error (0.6-1.3% for makima against 1.5-3.2% for a 3-point natural spline). On EV13's own fragment chromatograms (33,603 series, each skipped acquisition interpolated from the others at the demux geometry), makima and the 3-point spline underestimate the apex equally, by about 9-10% of height at the 2-3 points per peak width these runs have, against 11-12% for linear. Makima's real advantage is lower noise: 5-7% lower error over each peak's upper half. That, and its identifications with the Poisson refit, are the reasons to use it.

How the read got fast. A dotTrace profile showed the solve parallelized well while the wall time was set by the serial read. In it, the Thermo reader's precursor lookup took 22 s by itself: every DIA scan carries "Master Scan Number: 0", which matches no scan, and the lookup walked back to the first scan of the run for every spectrum to find that out. #4817 returns at once (40 s to 24 s here; plain msconvert of EV13 29.4 s to 21.9 s, identical mzML). Of the solve that remains, nearly half was MathNet building a general spline for every value; the closed form gives the same curve (within 1e-12 relative; every C++-parity gold test passes with it as the default) in two thirds of the time, and makima is also faster than MathNet's spline.

What the comparisons found

1. NNLS iteration limit (C# port bug) - #4805

On the Eclipse slice, 17 NNLS columns hit the 50-iteration cap; the C# port zeroed them, dropping 26 real peaks (up to 5.0e7) from 23 spectra. Keeping the last feasible solution, as C++ does, makes all 408 spectra match C++ at float32 precision.

2. Search-span regression from 2017 - #4814

C++'s SpectrumPeakExtractor is Skyline's TransitionBinner loop, line for line, but subtracts the largest bin half-width where Skyline subtracts the full width. Every bin above half the spectrum's highest m/z therefore accepted only part of its window: at the top, -10 to 0 ppm instead of +/-10. Against ground truth (fragments of identified peptides), the fix sends 92% of high-m/z fragment intensity to the correct window, against 69% before.

3. Why the search-span fix first looked like a loss

At Osprey's default 300,000-row training cap the corrected span lost 9.5% +/- 3.3% of precursors (five seeds), although its spectra and features are as good or better. The loss was the first-pass Percolator training, where about 1,000 positives drive the classifier and near-tied choices decide it. At 1.5M rows the corrected span is +1.0% and the seed spread falls from 6-11% to 1-3%. Filed as #4812; it is why every comparison above uses three seeds at 1.5M.

4. Thermo master-scan lookup - #4817

The speed section above: an O(n²) walk in the Thermo reader, 22 s of a 40 s read, found by profiling.
